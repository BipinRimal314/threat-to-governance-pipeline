# No prior work shows either evaluation shortcut fails

Nobody in the literature found so far has shown either claim directly, but two 2026 papers sit close enough that a reviewer will raise them first. For claim 1, the nearest is Li et al., who show that monitors trained on prompt-elicited reward hacking collapse on training-time hacking, a synthetic-versus-natural gap in one code domain ([arXiv 2604.23488](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23488)). For claim 2, the nearest is Zavrak, who shows that naive random splits inflate AUROC by up to 26 points on agent tool-call detection, and who also uses ATBench ([arXiv 2605.11053](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11053)). No source found reports a per-category ranking inversion between injected perturbations and labelled real trajectories, and none flags TRAIL as unfit for AUROC or uses "transfer beats in-domain" as a diagnostic. The novelty claim must therefore read "we found no prior report", because the search was roughly 22 calls per topic and not exhaustive. The fresh rerun weakens the headline statistics but not the finding. The comparison covers 4 shared categories (ASI01, ASI02, ASI06, ASI10), and Spearman rho is **-0.40 (exact p = 0.75)**, not -1.000. The per-category AUCs are unchanged: ASI02 is 0.51-0.52 synthetic against 0.82-0.91 real, and ASI06 is 0.92-0.98 synthetic against 0.80-0.91 real. On TRAIL, with 5 negatives, the Hanley-McNeil 95% intervals are about ±0.24 (0.577 gives 0.33-0.82; 0.690 gives 0.49-0.89), so the in-domain versus transfer gap is within noise. The paper should be framed as a cautionary workshop paper built on specific, honestly sized counterexamples, not as a demonstrated general law. The authors' own SSRN paper (6355658) must be cited and disclosed as prior work.

## What the evidence supports, and what it does not

Claim 1 has a lineage but no direct predecessor. Sommer and Paxson ([IEEE S&P 2010](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/5504793)), McHugh's critique of the DARPA evaluations ([PDF copy](https://www.cs.ucsb.edu/~kemm/courses/CS595/TestingIDSs/mchugh_ll_critique.pdf.gz)), and Arp et al. ([2010.09470](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.09470)) established decades ago that synthetic or simulated data produces optimistic and artefact-driven results. The control literature now says the same about attack elicitation. The new contribution is the measurement unit: a per-category ranking against labelled real trajectories. Honesty about size matters here. After the correction, there are 4 categories and one rank correlation of -0.40 with p = 0.75. With n = 4, the smallest possible exact two-sided p is 2/24 = 0.083, so even the published -1.000 could never have been significant. The paper should drop rank correlation as evidence and lead with the two per-category AUC gaps (ASI02, 0.51-0.52 against 0.82-0.91, and ASI06 in the opposite direction). There is also a mechanism explanation that strengthens the argument. The generator defines ASI02 to change only privilege features and to keep behavioural structure intact, so the blind spot exists by construction. BrowseSafe++ reports the same kind of shortcut risk from perturbation style ([post](https://perplexity.ai/hub/blog/browsesafe-plus-plus)).

Claim 2 has tools but no precedent. The Hanley-McNeil intervals show that 5 negatives cannot separate 0.577 from 0.690. Note that AUROC equals P(random positive outranks random negative), so the 97% positive prior drops out in theory ([Hanley and McNeil](https://doi.org/10.1148/radiology.143.1.7063747)). The defensible phrasing is that 5 negative traces make the rank statistic noisy and open to nuisance features such as trace length or GAIA versus SWE-Bench origin. "Base rate inflates AUROC" contradicts McDermott et al. ([2401.06091](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.06091)) and should be avoided. Axelsson's base-rate paper ([TISSEC 2000](https://www.profsandhu.com/cs6393_s20/p186-axelsson.pdf)) covers the opposite regime (rare attacks), so cite it only for "prevalence changes what a score means". The TRAIL dataset card helps: errors were partly induced, so the few negatives are an accident of curation and not a sampled benign class ([dataset card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/PatronusAI/TRAIL/blob/main/README.md)). TRAIL was designed for error localisation, not trace-level detection ([2505.08638](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.08638)), so the paper should say it uses the dataset outside its design intent.

## Gaps a reviewer will raise

The first gap is the data label. ATBench was built with rule and LLM filtering plus a human audit, and the abstract does not say the trajectories are real ([2604.02022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02022)). Calling it "real" is not defensible. Describe it as an independently constructed, human-audited, model-generated benchmark. This changes the claim to "our perturbation generator disagrees with a different generator plus labels", which is weaker and must be stated. The second gap is the shape of claim 1: a reviewer will say that a generator which touches only privilege features is a bug, not a general failure of synthetic injection. Answer it by naming the mechanism, and by showing ASI06, where synthetic scores higher than real, so the error runs in both directions. The third gap is the missing comparison on the same data. Zavrak shows content-aware features reach above 0.89 against a 0.64 metadata ceiling, so a behaviour-only detector needs a content-aware or LLM-monitor baseline, plus a trivial baseline (trace length, span count, source dataset, always-positive). Without those, "detector skill" and "artefact" cannot be separated on TRAIL. The fourth gap is statistics. Report Hanley-McNeil or bootstrap intervals for every AUC, add the 4-category correction openly, and drop the -1.000 figure everywhere, including in `paper/main.tex`. The fifth gap is reproducibility: the CMU-CERT loader code is lost, so CERT numbers cannot be rerun, and the TRAIL and Exp 3/8/9 result files were key-migrated and not re-run. Mark every CERT number as unreproduced, or remove it. The sixth gap is split protocol: random splits are the standard objection, so state that splits are task-disjoint. The seventh is scope. The work rests on one IsolationForest-family set of detectors and a 20-dimension schema, while the 2026 norm is LLM judges, and the OWASP ASI mapping rests mostly on grey literature (AgentThreatBench, AASTF drafts), so word it as a taxonomy exercise and not validated coverage.

## Conclusion

The defensible paper is narrower than the one the data first suggested. Its value is two worked counterexamples with honest uncertainty: a per-category synthetic profile that mis-ranks a category (ASI02) that real labels place among the best-detected, and an AUROC comparison on 5 negatives that cannot support any ordering of detectors. The weak -0.40 correlation is in the paper's favour as long as it is reported plainly, since a paper about overconfident evaluation cannot lean on a p of 0.75.

The strongest next step is cheap and checkable. Run a trivial-feature baseline on TRAIL, add an interval to every AUC, and run the one content-aware baseline on ATBench. If trace length alone matches the IsolationForest scores on TRAIL, claim 2 becomes a demonstration instead of a warning.
